Is a local LLM really 10× cheaper? A TCO VOFI review of Revolut's GLM move

By Dr. Lofi Dewanto · Also by the author: AI or Die

Revolut says moving 2,000 engineers from metered frontier APIs to self-hosted GLM on 56 Nvidia B200s cut its AI coding bill by about 90%. This report rebuilds that decision with the TCO VOFI method (Visualization of Financial Implications). It covers four real alternatives, three years, taxes, financing, salvage value, and the opportunity costs the headline leaves out.

The four options in plain words

Four ways to give 2,000 engineers an AI coding assistant, explained as ways to get around town.

ATaxi
Frontier API

Pay Anthropic or OpenAI for every word the AI reads or writes. The best models and nothing to run, but the highest price per word.

BCheaper taxi company
Managed open-weight API

The same free open model Revolut chose (GLM), run by another company that charges per word. Much cheaper than A, and still nothing to run yourself.

CLeased car
Self-hosted on rented GPUs

Rent AI chips (Nvidia B200) from a cloud provider and run GLM yourself. No big upfront purchase, but you need your own team to run and tune it.

DBuying the car
Self-hosted on owned GPUs

Buy the AI servers (about $4M upfront) and run everything yourself, including power, data-centre space and staff. This appears to be what Revolut did.

In one line: the big saving comes from moving from A's top-tier model to a cheaper open model (B, C or D). Whether you then rent it, lease chips or buy chips makes only a small difference.

    Why the headline comparison is weak

    Eight problems with "10× cheaper"

    The article compares one price per token with another. Gartner's TCO already went further than that, and your paper's critique of Gartner's TCO applies here even more strongly.

    1. Different models are being comparedIt sets a frontier closed model (Claude/Codex) against an open-weight model (GLM). Self-hosting should be measured against the same open weights on a managed API, which costs roughly ⅐ of frontier rates.
    2. Marginal cost presented as total costThe $0.30/M figure appears to cover running cost only. Hardware, the tuning team, power, colocation, observability and governance are left out.
    3. Cost accounting, not capital budgetingIt has no time value of money, no capital lock-up, no financing and no tax effects. Your paper calls this the main theoretical weakness of the classic TCO.
    4. No salvage value or obsolescenceB200s lose value quickly as newer GPUs arrive. Their residual value and depreciation schedule drive the result.
    5. Volumes are not equalThe old API bill was capped and the new bill is a flat rate. The alternatives have to be compared at the same token volume and growth path.
    6. Productivity is attributed to the wrong causeThe 60–70% gains arrived when the spending caps were removed. Uncapped API usage could have produced similar gains.
    7. Utilization and latency are ignoredThroughput of 200 tok/s by day and 800 tok/s at night means the cluster is saturated during work hours and idle afterwards. Both cost money: engineers wait by day, and capital sits unused at night.
    8. Self-reported and unauditedThe figures come from a single conference talk, the model versions differ between outlets, and no invoices or methodology were published.
    Method

    TCO VOFI in three steps

    The core question: how much poorer is the company after 3 years because of this decision, compared with simply investing the money?

    A classic TCO just adds up the bills. TCO VOFI (Visualization of Financial Implications, in German "Vollständiger Finanzplan") works like a bank statement for each option. It records every payment in the year it happens, adds interest and taxes, and looks at the balance at the end.

    Step 1Collect all payments

    What do we pay, and when? Purchase, running costs, staff, and the salvage value at the end. Plus the financial data: own funds, interest rates and tax rate.

    →
    Step 2Write a bank statement for each year

    Every option starts with the same own funds. Each year it pays its bills, earns or pays interest, and gets tax back on its costs. If the money runs out, a loan fills the gap. The result is the balance after year 3.

    →
    Step 3Compare with doing nothing

    The same own funds, simply invested, would have grown to a known amount. TCO VOFI = that amount − the option's final balance. It is the wealth the decision costs.

    Engine validated

    The calculator reproduces the original paper

    The same JavaScript engine that computes the Revolut case below was run on the eLearning example from Bensberg & Dewanto. It matches all four published values to the euro.

    Formulas used

    interestt = balt−1 × (rc if bal ≥ 0, else rd)
    taxbaset = int − opext − dept ± interestt
    taxt = s × taxbaset (negative = refund)
    balt = balt−1 ± interestt + int − outt − taxt
    EVopp = E × (1 + rc(1−s))T
    TCOVOFI = EVopp − balT

    E is the internal funds, set to the $ capex budget of the owned-hardware option so that every alternative starts with the same funds. s is the tax rate. Depreciation uses UK full expensing (100% in year 1) by default, with a linear option in the lab.

    Beyond cash

    Opportunity costs, explicit and hidden

    VOFI already prices the explicit opportunity cost: capital tied up in GPUs instead of earning interest, and the tax paid on the interest that is given up. The hidden opportunity costs below never appear on an invoice. They are converted to $ per year and passed through the same VOFI engine, which gives the extended TCO VOFI.     So what

    A better way to run this TCO

    1 · Compare like with like

    Measure self-hosted GLM against managed GLM, and frontier against frontier. Treat "open versus frontier" (a question of quality) and "own versus rent versus API" (a question of sourcing) as two separate decisions.

    2 · Use payments, not costs

    Build a VOFI with the actual timing of capex, opex, salvage, tax shields (UK full expensing is worth a lot here) and the firm's real creditor and debtor rates.

    3 · Fix the volume, then vary it

    Model every option at the same token path, then sweep the volume to find the break-even. Revolut is well above it. Most companies are not.

    4 · Price utilization in both directions

    Daytime saturation costs engineer time and nighttime idle costs capital. Add burst capacity (rented GPUs or API overflow) and fill nights with batch work such as evals, fraud models and data labelling.

    5 · Treat productivity as the dominant variable

    Revolut's AI tools cost a few million dollars a year, while engineering payroll is about $400M. A quality gap of 1–2% outweighs any infrastructure saving, so measure acceptance and rework rates per model.

    6 · Keep the option to switch

    Prefer rented or reserved GPUs with short terms and a model-agnostic router. This keeps the bargaining power that self-hosting gives without locking capital into one GPU generation.
